Every computer in this museum that touches a body points the same way: *inward*. The [P300 speller](../exhibits/p300-speller/) reads brainwaves. The [IBVA headband](../exhibits/ibva/) digitizes your EEG. The [Kay Visi-Pitch with electroglottograph](../exhibits/electroglottograph/) watches your vocal folds move through the tissue of your own neck. The [Synapse headband](../exhibits/relax-stress-reduction/) flies a balloon by how relaxed your brow is. They are all instruments of listening — amplifiers pointed at the machine that happens to be you, trying to pull a usable signal out of the body.

The [Cochlear Nucleus 22](../exhibits/cochlear-nucleus-22/) is the first artifact in the whole place that points the other way. It writes back in.

## No speaker. No sound. Just your nerve.

A cochlear implant has no loudspeaker, because there's nobody listening to a speaker — the "output" is fired straight at the brain. A digital speech processor sits outside and does the analysis: it picks up sound through a microphone, figures out the frequency content across a handful of channels, and decides which electrodes to fire. Then it sends a coded electrical pulse train across a transcutaneous link — through the skin, by radio — to a 22-electrode array threaded into the cochlea. Those 22 electrodes stimulate the auditory nerve directly.

The machine doesn't hand you words. It hands your nerve a pattern of spikes. And this is the part that makes it the strangest and most moving interface this museum keeps: *the machine bets that the rest of the job gets done by you* — by your brain, rewiring itself.

## Neuroplasticity as the interaction contract

Think about what a normal interface asks of its operator. A keyboard asks you to co-ordinate fingers. A glove asks you to move your hand. The Nucleus 22 asks your *brain to learn to hear*. When the electrode array is switched on for the first time, what the newly implanted person experiences is not speech — it's noise, pops, raw electrical patter that has no meaning. The system only becomes language over months, as the auditory cortex discovers that particular patterns of spikes correspond to the sounds around them. The interface isn't finished. The user's own nervous system has to finish it.

That's the reverse of everything else in this building. The [P300 speller](../exhibits/p300-speller/) measures the brainwaves you already make and maps them to letters. The implant doesn't measure anything — it *presents* a stimulus and trusts the brain to learn it. This is an output device whose output is a hypothesis, and the hypothesis is tested and refined by years of a human relearning what sound means.

The lineage goes back to Graeme Clark's program at the University of Melbourne, which refused to give up on multi-channel stimulation through the long, uncertain 1970s — and, commercialized through Cochlear Limited, won FDA approval for the Nucleus 22 in 1985. The 1989 [Mini Speech Processor](../exhibits/cochlear-nucleus-22/) is the moment the whole thing became wearable: the DSP analysis shrank to a body-worn unit, the "computer" that at first needed a cart now clipped on like a pager.

## The quiet kind of miracle

I keep returning to one detail. In the museum I love, half these machines flopped, failed to find a market, or were surpassed — and I love them *for* that, for ambition that outran its moment. The cochlear implant is the opposite case and it's worth holding the two of them together. The P300 speller changed lives for locked-in patients, and the DataGlove became a holiday toy. Both are the same story — humans reaching toward new ways to talk to machines. The cochlear is the version of that story where the machine went *into* the body and the body answered.

The extra-electronic input is a [button-sized chip that holds identity in steel](../exhibits/ibutton/), and the machine that [listens to your neck](../exhibits/electroglottograph/) reads while this one writes. But the cochlear's true family tree is stranger. It is the museum's only cyborg in the most literal, medically regulated sense — a computer as an output sense organ, an interface that only works if you're brave enough to let it change what you are. No other artifact here asks the user to become a different person to make sense of it. That's not a flaw. That's the design.
