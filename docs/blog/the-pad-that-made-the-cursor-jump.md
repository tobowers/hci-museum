Every trackpad you've ever owned has been trained in the same gospel: you slide, and the cursor slides with you, relative motion. First finger here, cursor there; drag, pause, drag again. The whole grammar of the modern pointing surface is one long, smooth translation of your fingerprint across a plate into a pointer across a screen.

The [Xerox Cat](../exhibits/xerox-cat/) (1980) believed the opposite. It is the near-extinct ancestor of every laptop trackpad, and it worked backwards.

The Cat — the name stands for **c**apacitance-**a**ctivated **t**ransducer — was a circular, touch-sensitive pad set into the keyboard of the Xerox 860 word-processing workstation. It sat to the right of the keys, a little disk you could reach without leaving the typing row, offered as a gentler way to select a stretch of text for copying, deleting, inserting, or moving. And its pointing model was *absolute*. You didn't push the cursor toward your destination. You *placed* your finger on the Circle, and the cursor teleported to the corresponding spot on screen — left edge on the pad meant left edge of the document, bottom on the pad meant bottom of the page. Then, to refine where you landed, the cursor's speed scaled with how hard you pressed. Absolute position to get close, pressure to fine-tune.

Think about what that means as an interface. The mouse is a creature of *motion* — the TrackPoint in this museum's own keyboard is its immobile cousin, reading force instead of travel. But the Cat is neither. It is a pointer that does not believe in travel at all. The cursor does not glide over the territory between you and your goal; it **arrives**. One touch, and you are there. It reads your finger's *place*, not your finger's *path*.

The strangeness bites hardest when you hold it up against everything the era believed a pointing device was for. Xerox spent the late seventies inventing the desktop metaphor, the windows, the icons — and here, embedded in a workhorse word-processing keyboard, was a pointing surface that skipped the whole "walking the cursor across the screen" ritual. It was the fastest possible way to say *that* bit of text, there, that paragraph, copy it. The very fictions that make a desktop feel like a desk were, for a moment, scheduled over by a fingertip that just pointed.

Why records remember the Cat as "one of the first, if not the first, touchpads on a computer" is exactly because of how ordinary it now looks. More than a decade before anyone called a laptop trackpad a trackpad, Xerox had already shipped the fundamental physics — a capacitive sheet that senses a finger without moving parts, that needs two hands on screen work and one hand doing the pointing with no mechanical travel and nothing to clean. That is the direct lineage of the trackpad. Your laptop's pad is the descendant of this circular disk.

But we did not get the Cat's mind. We got the slide. The relative-motion world won, because it generalizes — it lets the same plane serve an infinite screen by sweeping, and because it feels *continuous*, like steering. The absolute model only makes sense when the surface is small and the destination is reachable in one suppressed chord. Almost nobody does absolute-position touchpads anymore. They feel wrong now, the cursor's abrupt teleport unsettling, because we learned to think of pointing as gliding.

There is a small poetry in that loss. The Cat is the "pointer that jumps," a chapter in a museum that already keeps the pointer-that-doesn't-move (TrackPoint, reading force not travel) and the pointer-that-lifts-off-the-desk (the ultrasonic Fly Mouse, which only works airborne). Three answers to the same ancient question — *how does the hand tell the machine where?* — and the Cat gave the loudest, most economical answer of all: stop describing the route, and just *be* there.

The disk itself turns up in the 860's keyboard diagram as key #27, a little unlabeled circle beside the letters. It filled a genuinely unrepresented corner of this museum — the museum's only capacitive-touch pointing grammar — and it is foundational to the surface you are very likely reading this on.
