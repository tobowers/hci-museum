![A Kodak Komstar laser computer-output-microfilm recorder at CERN, 1981](../assets/wiki/kodak-komstar-cern-1981.jpg)
*The Komstar on-line to CERN's central IBM mainframes in March 1981, installed to cut the Computer Centre's paper consumption. CERN PhotoLab, CC BY 4.0.*

Here is a machine whose job was to make its own output disappear.

In 1977 the [Kodak Komstar](../exhibits/kodak-komstar/) sat at the end of a mainframe's channel and took exactly what a line printer would have taken: an ordinary stream of report text. Upstream, nothing changed; the program believed it was printing. Downstream, everything did. Instead of ink on paper, a laser drew each character as a 7 × 9 dot matrix onto dry-process film — 105 mm fiche or 16 mm roll — at roughly 120,000 characters a second, some twenty times an impact printer's speed. The reams a data centre shipped came back as a handful of cards the size of a postcard, each holding a couple of hundred page-images.

I want to dwell on the part that isn't engineering, because it's the part this museum cares about.

## Two interventions, one for each end

Every output device intervenes somewhere, and where it intervenes tells you who it works for. The Komstar intervened at both ends of a wire carrying nothing but text.

At the machine end, it attacked *throughput*. The problem was not how a report looked but how long it took to come out. Twenty-times-the-speed is a data-centre argument — fewer machines, no ribbon, nobody lifting a box of fanfold. Nothing about the report changed; only how fast it could be born.

At the human end, it changed *gesture*, and did so without asking.

## The reading changed shape

A report on fanfold is read by the forearm: you lift a stack, flip pages, keep a thumb in the appendix while you flip back to the summary. The page matches a body.

A report on fiche has no forearm reading. It threads into a viewer and you slide a magnified window across a grid of tiny pages, in the machine's own ordering smuggled into the layout. Finding *page 143 of appendix B* becomes a hunt on a glass stage. The wrist flipped; now the fingertips scan.

The strange thing is that the data centre couldn't see this cost. From where the Komstar stood, output got cheaper, faster and smaller — strictly better. The only person who paid was the reader, who was never in the loop.

## The pair that brackets the era

The museum already holds the other half of this world. The [DASA Mark 18 reader-printer](../exhibits/dasa-mark-18-reader-printer/) is the reading end: a console that projects a frame of film and, on demand, prints it back to paper. It takes roll film, fiche and aperture cards in one box — a quiet admission that the film world had three incompatible lookup gestures, and the reader had to perform all three. The Komstar is the writing end. Together they close the loop: data onto film in one building, read off film in another, back to paper on request so someone can hold it.

Neither machine is here alone; each is the other's absence. A note about the reader alone would explain how the reading felt and leave the writing invisible. The Komstar alone would explain how the writing got cheap and leave the reading unexamined. So this is a note about the gap between them.

That gap has a shape. The film world's three media differ not by what they hold but by *how you find one thing in a big set*: roll by winding, fiche by grid-walk, an aperture card by its punched holes. It is the same trio of lookup gestures every file system has had since, made of film and plastic. The Mark 18's three inputs are a primitive retrieval taxonomy; the Komstar filled it.

## Why it stayed, and why it faded

The film era lasted because it was cheap and honest. Film packs a lot into little space and needs no power; a card pulled from a drawer in 1981 still reads under a lens. That is the same virtue I keep finding at exactly the moment it stops looking like one: [the newspaper that was a database](../blog/the-newspaper-was-a-database.md), [the plates you carried by the handle](../blog/the-database-you-carried-by-the-handle.md), the fiche on the glass — all make the data a thing with a body.

It faded because the body is also the limit. You cannot grep a fiche, mail one frame to a colleague, sort a drawer by a query, or link one frame to another. The moment the screen got cheap enough to be a page, the film world had no argument left: its whole advantage was density, and density has to be walked.

The Komstar is worth keeping because it did one thing a screen never does. It took a report and made it small enough for a pocket — and did not hide that getting it back out would mean using your hands.
