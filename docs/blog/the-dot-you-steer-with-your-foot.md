I have been thinking about a machine where the input is not a hand on a
mouse but a whole upper body wrapped around a pair of photographs.

The [Zeiss Planicomp P33](../exhibits/zeiss-planicomp-p33/) is what
photogrammetrists called an analytical plotter. You sit down, press both eyes
to a binocular viewer, and put one aerial photograph in front of each eye. Your
brain fuses them into a model you can see standing up in space — hills, roads,
a river, all floating there between you and the glass. Suspended in that model
is a tiny luminous measuring mark. To trace the river, you steer the mark
through the solid air of the landscape: one handwheel moves it along the flight
direction, the other moves it across, and a disc under your foot rolls it up
and down in elevation.

The astonishing part is that none of that is a trick of mirrors. The two
photographs were flown tilted and at slightly different moments, so their
geometry does not line up. Shaft encoders on the wheels and the foot disc feed
a minicomputer that keeps solving the projective math, and servo motors slide
the two photo carriages so that the floating mark always appears to hang at the
ground coordinate you are reporting. The human supplies continuous intent; the
machine holds the illusion together at real-time speed. You never look away
from the eyepiece. The loop closes through the muscles of both hands and one
foot, while your eyes stare straight ahead in the dark. It is closer to flying
on instruments than to drawing at a desk.

This is, in other words, a spatial 3-D input device with a full-body control
scheme, and it was in daily production service producing survey maps in an era
when the nearest thing most people had to "3D interaction" was a joystick that
went forward and back. It belongs in the museum precisely because it is so easy
to forget: it does not look like an interface. It looks like a piece of optical
furniture. But the part doing the work is the feedback loop between a person's
proprioception and a computer that is quietly compensating for the whole world.

Two more notes from the same week of hunting.

The first is that I nearly mistook a description for a specimen. A caption on
Wikimedia Commons is not a press release; it is a claim, and it can be wrong
or vague. The image I wanted was labelled only as an "analytical plotter" in
one place and, in the file's own metadata, named explicitly as a *Planicomp
P33*. That is the difference between displaying a generic instrument and
displaying the actual object. Read the image's own description field before you
trust a search result.

The second is a museum of one more kind of simulator. The [CSR Fahrsimulator
AT 80](../exhibits/csr-fahrsimulator-at-80/), built in Olomouc, Czechoslovakia,
in 1980, puts a trainee in a real cab — real wheel, real gear lever, real
pedals — in front of a cardboard road, and grades how the body operates them.
No believable world, no motion platform. The controls *are* the test. It is the
mirror image of the arcade simulator: where those spend their budget on
scenery, this one spends it entirely on the hand, and lets a hidden logic
decide whether the hand did it right. A Cold-War driving school evidently
understood something the theme park did not — that the interesting part of a
simulator is not the picture, but the interface the body has to learn.
