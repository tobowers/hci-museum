Every keyboard in this museum has something in common: it assumes the hands do the thinking. From the [space-cadet keyboard](../exhibits/space-cadet-keyboard/) to the [SynthAxe](../exhibits/synthaxe/) to the [OASYS thumb-shift](../exhibits/oasys-thumbshift-keyboard/), the interface lives at fingertip, and the whole drama of input is an argument about *fingers* — how many, how fast, how many times each key can be reused. It took Moog, of all people, to ask the question nobody else was asking: what if the hands weren't the point at all?

The [Moog Taurus I](../exhibits/moog-taurus/) (1975) is a synthesizer with no keyboard. None. Not a small one, not a cleverly disguised one. Instead of a keyboard, it has a spinet-organ-style pedal board — thirteen pedals — and you choose the pitch with your feet while both hands go and play something else. The bass line is performed with the lower body. It is the museum's only keyboard-less synthesizer, and its only instrument where feet select pitch.

## The body as a split channel

I want to sit with how strange that really is, because it quietly inverts a two-hundred-year assumption. On a piano, on an organ, on every synthesizer in this museum before the Taurus, the hands are the entire input. Both hands, all ten fingers, fighting over the same scarce real estate. A keyboardist is a person who has learned to be two people at once — a left hand and a right hand that don't always agree — because the machine demands that the whole musical argument happen above the wrist.

The Taurus was built for the opposite situation. It came out of Moog's never-released Constellation concept: a rig where one musician could do the work of several, hands on one keyboard while the feet carried a bass line on the pedals below. The interaction is a radical reallocation of a musical channel across the body. Instead of asking the hands to do everything, the Taurus gives the lower limbs their own continuous, pitch-bearing job — and leaves the hands free. The classic-configuration use on Emerson, Lake & Palmer's *Brain Salad Surgery* was exactly that: a bass player's feet doing work that would otherwise demand a whole other hand.

There is something almost athletic about it. A keyboard is a desk instrument — you sit and you type at it. The Taurus is a stance instrument. You stand, your weight shifts from pedal to pedal, and the machine hears a line of music rising out of your balance.

## The window you're not allowed to touch

The detail that makes me love it is the plastic window. The Taurus's sound-shaping controls — the filter, the envelopes, the whole voice — sit behind a removable plastic cover so they *cannot be adjusted mid-song*. The machine is deliberately set up, then played feet-first, without reaching down to tweak. It is a design that trusts the choreography more than the knob-twiddling. The interface is a promise: you've done your configuring, now get up and play the thing with your body.

That is a genuinely rare philosophy in an era when every instrument was racing toward *more* controls, *more* immediacy, *more* hands-on everything. The Taurus bet the opposite way — that the interface's job was to get out of the lower body's way.

## The controller with no brain

The strangest coda is the variant that proves the whole idea. The model 343C Taurus II Controller shipped CV and gate outputs and *no synthesizer module at all*. It was a pure foot-input device — a standalone pitch/gate controller that read your lower-body gestures and sent control voltages to other gear. No sound of its own. Just the pedals, abstracted into a reusable input surface.

That is the clearest statement of what the Taurus really is. It isn't a bass synthesizer that happens to be played by feet; it is an input device — a lower-body channel — that happens to have been shipped inside an instrument. Pull the sound source away and the interface survives on its own, still perfectly meaningful. The pedals were always the point, the synth just the first thing they drove. It is an early, honest example of splitting the input surface from the sound source, decades before "controllers" were a recognized category of instrument.

## The lower body's turn

I'll be honest about the framing, because the museum loves this exact thing: the Taurus is not just a clever instrument, it is an argument about which parts of the body deserve to be interfaces at all. The museum already keeps the lower body as an input channel in play — [Stompin'](../exhibits/stompin/), [LifeRower](../exhibits/liferower/), [Surf Champ](../exhibits/surf-champ/) — but those are games, exertion translated into direction or speed. The Taurus is the musical one: the lower body doing continuous, expressive, *pitch-bearing* work while the hands do something else entirely.

Progressive rock built whole careers on it — Genesis, Rush, Yes, Pink Floyd, The Police. A generation of bass players learned to carry a line with their feet because Moog decided the hands could be trusted with the rest of the band. It is one of the museum's purest demonstrations that an interface is not a desk or a rectangle or a keyboard — sometimes it is simply the way you choose to stand.
