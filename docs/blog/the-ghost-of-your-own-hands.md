In 1987, Yamaha built a piano that could play itself. Not a synthesizer
pretending to be a piano, not a speaker playing back a recording — a real
acoustic piano, with real hammers and real strings, with a floppy disk drive
bolted onto the side. The [Yamaha Disklavier](../exhibits/disklavier/) had a
ghost inside it.

The mechanism is worth sitting with. Fiber-optic sensors on the keys, hammers,
and pedals capture a performance — every touch, every strike, every pedal
shift — and store it on a 3.5-inch floppy in Yamaha's E-SEQ format, a forerunner
of the MIDI file. Then, on playback, electromechanical solenoids physically move
those same keys. The hammers strike the strings. No speakers, no samples, no
amplification. Just the piano, playing the piano.

Why does this belong in a museum of human-computer interaction? Because it is
output with a body. We are used to machines that speak through speakers and draw
on screens. The Disklavier reached into the physical world and moved an
instrument — one humans have played for three centuries — through the same
mechanisms a person's fingers use. It is the musical cousin of the little
[pen plotter](../exhibits/commodore-1520-plotter/) whose paper whips back and
forth while a solenoid hammers the pen down: output as mechanical ceremony. But
the plotter does a machine's drawing. The Disklavier does a human's act, at the
human's instrument, with the human's exact motions. The interface isn't the
point. The body is.

The lineage matters here. The 19th-century pneumatic player piano played itself
from punched paper rolls, but it had no ears — it could only reproduce what
someone had punched into the roll. The Disklavier added the other half of the
loop: it could listen. That is what changed everything, and it is what composers
eventually exploited.

The defining moment came in 1989, when Jean-Claude Risset wrote *Duet For One
Pianist* in Marseille. The premise is exactly as strange as it sounds. A pianist
plays. An algorithm transforms the notes — mirrors them, delays them,
arpeggiates them — and sends them back to the same piano in real time. The
piano answers. The performer duets with a machine that is literally the same
instrument, played by the ghost of their own hands. Risset and pianist Simon
Bolzinger built the DKompose library in Max/MSP around the idea, and the lineage
runs forward to Kyle Gann's three Disklaviers tuned to microtonal intervals and
Dan Tepfer's *Natural Machines*.

There is a physical texture to the interaction that software could never
capture, and it comes from the machine's own body: the delay. Between the moment
MIDI data reaches the solenoids and the moment the hammer strikes the string,
about a quarter of a second passes. A human pianist sight-reading the same
music would never be that late. The lag is part of the duet — you hear your own
playing transformed, arriving a beat late, from a body that shares your bench.
The machine is not faster than you. It is just *other*.

The Disklavier went on to become a standard conservatory tool, and its network
experiments were genuinely ahead of their time. In 1997, Ryuichi Sakamoto
transmitted a performance to thousands of locations. The Minnesota International
Piano-e-Competition synchronized Disklavier PROs on two continents so a jury
member in Japan could experience a pianist playing live in St. Paul —
telepresence for music, twenty years before the word was in common use. The
[Radio Drum](../exhibits/radio-drum/) already sits in this museum for the same
reason: David A. Jaffe wrote a seventy-minute concerto in which the drum
conducted a Disklavier grand. Gesture on one side, a real piano answering on the
other.

I keep the Disklavier because of the duet. Most of the machines in this
collection are input devices or output devices — they listen, or they speak.
The Disklavier is the rarer thing: a machine that listens and then plays. A
collaborator that shares its body with you. There is something quietly profound,
and a little eerie, about a piano that can play your hands back to you — while
still being the same piano you learned on.

The ghost doesn't replace the performer. The ghost is the performer, sent back
through the instrument. That is an interface idea with no descendants in
software, and I'm not sure it needs any.
