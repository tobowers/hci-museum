To send a fax in 1975, you wrapped your document around a drum.

Not metaphorically. The [Mufax Courier](../exhibits/mufax-courier/) was a
portable facsimile transceiver from Muirhead & Co., the Scottish firm that had
been building drum-based fax machines since the 1940s. You took your page,
rolled it around a rotating cylinder, dialed the far party on an ordinary
telephone, and pressed the handset down into the rubber cups of an acoustic
coupler built into the lid. The drum turned, a carriage traversed its length,
and the machine converted the image line-by-line into audio tones that went
down the phone line. On the far end, another drum — spinning in synchronized
lockstep — marked a fresh sheet of paper.

There was no screen, no menu, no readout. The interface was almost entirely
physical: drum, coupling, a handful of switches, and a very long stretch of
trust that the two ends were actually in phase.

But the Courier had one detail that is the reason I keep coming back to it. On
the sending side sat a single lamp. And that lamp carried exactly one bit of
information from the far machine.

If the receiving end was getting a bad scan — a shaky line, a noisy phone
connection, an out-of-sync drum — the lamp lit up. That was all. It was not a
diagnostic. It was not a percentage or a graph or a message. It was a light
that told you, mid-transmission, that the machine on the other end of the
telephone line was not hearing you cleanly. 'One-bit instant feedback from the
far end if an unsatisfactory reception is occurring,' as the documentation
put it.

Think about how strange that is. In 1975, a fax machine was shipping with a
remote telemetry channel. The far machine's health was being rendered to the
operator as a single glowing lamp, live, while the drum turned. You did not
need to walk to the other room. You did not need to call. You glanced at the
light and you *knew* the far end was struggling — and you could do something
about it before you wasted the whole page.

Today that lamp is everywhere, and we do not even see it. The status light on
your modem. The blinking LEDs on your router. The spinner on your browser that
quietly tells you the server has stopped answering. The entire modern language
of connection quality — green for good, amber for poor, red for gone — is
descended from the idea that a single lamp can carry the state of a distant
machine. The Mufax Courier got there in 1975, with a bulb and a drum.

The museum's telephony family is full of machines that made the ritual of
remote communication physical. The [Novation CAT](../exhibits/novation-cat/)
was the modem you fed a telephone, listening for the squeal and pressing the
handset into rubber cups. The [TI Silent 700](../exhibits/ti-silent-700/) put
those same cups in a briefcase, dialing up CompuServe over heat-sensitive
paper. The [Hayes Smartmodem](../exhibits/hayes-smartmodem/) replaced the
whole ceremony with the AT command and its own little bank of status LEDs. The
Mufax sits at the front of that line, but it has something the others do not:
the fax's answer to "is the other side still with me?" was a lamp that meant
exactly one thing, aimed at a person who could still reach out and fix it.

There is a particular tenderness in that one-bit lamp. It is the whole remote
relationship compressed to a yes-or-no — the far machine is not abstract and
far away, it is a colleague who just told you, through a bulb, that the line is
bad. You cannot get much closer to the other end of a telephone line than
that.
