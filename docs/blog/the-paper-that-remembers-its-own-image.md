Every screen in this museum has a temper. A [Talaria](../exhibits/ge-talaria/) paints
its image with oil and light and forgets it a thirtieth of a second later. A
[Commodore 1520](../exhibits/commodore-1520-plotter/) finishes a drawing and hands you a
sheet of ink to protect from the world. Even the [Ferranti-Packard
flip-disc](../exhibits/ferranti-packard-flipdot/), the display that remembered itself,
holds its picture by flipping little discs over like dominoes — each one a yes or a no
that stays put because it's been physically turned.

And then there is the one output in the museum that never tried to be a display at all.

In the mid-1970s, Nicholas Sheridon at Xerox PARC built a sheet of transparent silicone
rubber and filled it with millions of microscopic plastic beads — each one half white,
half black, floating in its own little bubble of oil so it was free to spin. He called
the thing *Gyricon*, from the Greek *gyros*, "to turn." When a voltage swept across the
sheet, every bead tumbled in its pocket to present one side or the other. A pattern of
voltages arranged the beads into letters and pictures. Then the voltages stopped.

And the picture stayed.

![A rolled Gyricon sheet — flexible, thin, readable in bright light, a paper surrogate rather than a glowing screen.](../assets/wiki/gyricon-roll.jpg)

## Not a screen, a page

Here is the thing Sheridon was trying to make: not a display, but *paper*. Gyricon was
designed to be thin, flexible, unbreakable, and readable in bright ambient light —
sun on the beach, a bedside lamp, no backlight, no phosphor, no refresh scan. The beads
were drawn to a picture once and then simply stopped turning. It is the closest thing in
this museum to a book that can rewrite itself, and the direct physical ancestor of every
e-reader you have ever held.

Compare that to what most computer output *is*. A [Honeywell
Visicorder](../exhibits/honeywell-visicorder/) burns a trace because it wants to show you
what a signal was doing right now. A [Solari split-flap](../exhibits/solari-split-flap-display/)
drops a letter onto a drum. A flip-disc flips its discs. Each of these is a machine that
*drew* you something and then, at best, held its breath until the picture changed.

Gyricon never held its breath. It had no refresh to lose. The picture wasn't painted and
maintained — it was *assembled once, physically*, by beads that decided which color to show
and then committed. The image didn't need a scan, an electron gun, or a pen. It needed
nothing. The sheet remembered its own image because the image was the arrangement of the
beads, and the beads were the sheet.

## The medium is the interface

That is what makes Gyricon the cleanest realization of "the medium is the interface" in
the entire output family. A CRT is a vacuum tube with a story to tell. A plotter is a
conveyor for a pen. A flip-disc is a toggling curtain. Gyricon is neither a machine nor a
canvas — it is a *substrate*, and the substrate *is* the output. The beads don't display
the information; they become it. Hold up the sheet and the information is right there in
the material, exactly as a printed word sits in the ink of a page, exactly as a photograph
sits in the silver of the paper.

Sheridon's project never conquered the world on its own terms — it was spun out and later
largely superseded by the electrophoretic ink we call E Ink, which shares Gyricon's core
premise of physically rearranging charged particles into a persistent image. But that
premise is the quiet revolution. Every Kindle, every e-reader, every display that asks to
be read in sunlight rather than watched in the dark, owes its body to this sheet of rubber
full of spinning beads.

The rest of the museum's outputs have an opinion about your attention. Gyricon is the one
that just sits there — a page, holding its image, waiting to be read. It is the only
computer output that could plausibly be rolled up and stuck in a bookcase and forgotten,
and still remember exactly what it was showing when you come back for it.
