This museum keeps a strange family portrait of one idea wearing different uniforms: a machine-tool that [asks the machinist questions](../exhibits/heidenhain-tnc-110/) instead of demanding G-code, a [lab oscilloscope](../exhibits/tektronix-7854/) with a keyboard bolted on for waveform math, a [car dashboard](../exhibits/buick-gcc/) that replaced knobs with a touchscreen. All of them are the same moment — a computer's brains sliding into a machine that does physical work, and the interface turning from a dial into a program. The heiress nobody ever photographs is the oven.

The Amana Touchmatic Radarange, from Amana, Iowa, is what happened when that crossing reached the most ordinary surface a machine can occupy: the kitchen counter. Until 1975 a microwave was operated the way a furnace was — a knob, a timer, a dial you turned until it pointed at a number. The RR-6, shipped that year, quietly took the knob off and put a microprocessor behind a membrane keypad. It is, as far as I can tell, the first computing appliance most households ever owned.

What makes it a museum piece rather than a trivia fact is the shape of the interaction. Pressing the Touchmatic's keys was not turning a dial faster; it was *writing a short program*. You keyed in a cook time, a power level, a defrost stage — sometimes several stages in sequence — and the machine held the whole thing and executed it against the physical process of heating food. The oven couldn't bend a knob, so it learned to take commands. It was a miniature command line to a heat engine, wrapped in avocado-colored consumer packaging.

The digital ritual was the point. You composed a sequence on the keypad, the display echoed it back to confirm, the magnetron ran through the program, the clock counted down, and then the beep — the beep that every modern appliance inherited. A mechanical timer could express one operation. The microprocessor could express *defrost-then-cook*, a timed hold, a probe-triggered temperature stop. That difference — a machine that could chain operations it hadn't been built for — is exactly what separates a mechanism from a computer.

I want to be careful not to over-glorify it. This was not a computer for anyone to program in the sense a hobbyist programs an IMSAI. There was no user code, no branching, no imagination — you keyed in what you wanted and the oven obeyed, and a wrong keypress was an error light, not a teachable moment. It was computing reduced to its most obedient, least curious form. That is precisely why it matters. This is the boundary crossing at the moment it stopped being heroic and became domestic — the machine's brains did not have to do anything grand, only replace a dial without anyone having to learn anything.

The honesty I owe the era: this was not a better world. It was a weirder one. Here was the raw idea — *a thing that cooks can be told what to do by pressing keys* — arriving on the most ordinary surface of the house, in a decade when the same idea was, elsewhere in this museum, being used to make an [etch-a-sketch draw animation](../exhibits/etch-a-sketch-animator/) and to [ask a machinist questions](../exhibits/heidenhain-tnc-110/). The kitchen got the quietest version, and the quietest version was the one that won, because a microwave is the computer almost nobody ever has to think about.

The collection already keeps the interrogator, the instrument, and the dashboard. The oven is the proof that the idea didn't need a specialist or a hero — it only needed to sit somewhere a family cooked dinner, count down, and beep. Every modern oven, washing machine, and coffee maker with a keypad and a little digital clock is a great-grandchild of that Avocado-Iowa wager.

It is, I admit, the least glamorous computer in the building. I like it for exactly that reason. Somewhere between a [flight-deck color tube](../exhibits/collins-efis-glass-cockpit/) and a [chess-playing robot arm](../exhibits/novag-robot-adversary/), there is a machine whose entire claim to history is that it made programming feel like setting a timer. That is a real interface, and it changed more kitchens than the hero machines ever changed labs.
