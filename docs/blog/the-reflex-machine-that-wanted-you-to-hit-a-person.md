In 1974, on the border between the electromechanical carnival game and the video
arcade, Brunswick sold a two-player coin-op simply called *Karate*. It was a reflex
tester — not really a game, and definitely not a video game — and its interface was
one of the strangest a computer has ever worn: a near life-size printed combatant you
were supposed to hit.

Each player stood before a painted figure of a karate fighter. Ten incandescent bulbs
were sunk invisibly behind the print at different attack and defense points — a head,
a chest, an arm, a leg. When a point lit up, the first person to physically strike
their combatant there scored. A pseudo-random sequence advanced the next target. That
was the entire interaction. No joystick, no buttons in the modern sense, no screen to
read. The machine lit a spot on a person, and you punched that spot faster than your
opponent did. Your score was how quick your body was.

## The interface is a reaction time

Here's the thing I keep turning over: this machine wasn't testing your aim or your
strategy. It was testing your reflexes, and it made that explicit by making the body
the only thing that mattered. A whack-a-mole asks you to hit a hole. This asked you
to hit a *person* — or rather, to be fast enough that the *distance between the light
turning on and your fist arriving* is the smallest it can be. The interface reduced
the whole human-computer exchange to a single physical quantity, and it measured it
with nothing but switches and a counter.

That's a genuinely different thing from the two arcade ancestors the museum already
keeps. [Heavyweight Champ](../exhibits/heavyweight-champ/), two years later, is also a
no-CPU machine — pure discrete TTL logic, no microprocessor — but it's a *game* with
two gloves and a referee's count, a little drama of boxing. [Street
Fighter](../exhibits/sf1-pneumatic/) traded gloves for pneumatic pads that read how
hard you punched, but it's still a game with rounds and a winner. Karate is neither: it
has no winner in the way we mean it, just a faster striker. It's a carnival reflex
meter wearing a costume of violence.

## No software, just gates

There's a kinship worth honoring between Karate and Heavyweight Champ, and it's the
no-CPU fact. Inside Karate ran combinational logic only — 7400-series gates, BCD
counters, a self-reversing counter for the pseudo-random target order, 555 timers for
the lamp flashes. If you wanted to change the game, you did not edit code; you rewired
gates. The "computer" here is a tangle of transistors arranged to make a light jump
around a printed body. The [Heavyweight Champ
note](../blog/the-lost-arcade-game-that-had-no-software/) made the point that a game
with no software is, in a sense, un-recoverable once the cabinets are gone — there is
no ROM to dump. Karate is even more lost: no surviving unit appears in the collector
census, and all that remains is its patent and this diagram.

## Why it belongs here

I love Karate because it is HCI with the skin still on. So much of this museum's era
is people reaching, awkwardly, toward gentler or stranger ways to talk to machines —
gloves, voices, brainwaves, breath. And here is a machine that just wants you to hit
the person on the card where the light is. It's not sentimental and it's not dignified,
and that's exactly why it shouldn't be a footnote. It is the honest, muscle-bound end
of the input spectrum, the ancestor of every [foot-stomping
grid](../exhibits/stompin/) and force-sensing punch pad that followed — before video
got a chance to dress it up in metaphor.

The reflex machine didn't ask you to think. It asked you to be fast, and it called
your body the interface. In a museum full of machines that wanted to be understood,
Karate just wanted to be hit.
