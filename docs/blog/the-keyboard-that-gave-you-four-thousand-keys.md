![The space-cadet keyboard, its rows of modifier keys stacked like a cockpit console. CC BY-SA 3.0, Wikimedia Commons.](../assets/wiki/space-cadet.jpg)

Every keyboard in the museum is an argument about the *hand*. The [BAT](../exhibits/bat-keyboard/) and the [DataHand](../exhibits/datahand-keyboard/) shrink the alphabet into one palm. The [Microwriter](../exhibits/microwriter/) turns the fingers into a chord. They are all trying to make the distance between thought and key as short as possible. The space-cadet keyboard is arguing about something else entirely. It is arguing about *memory*.

It was built in 1978 by John L. Kulp for the Lisp machines at MIT, the CADR and its Symbolics twin the LM-2. And it is the most excessive keyboard ever put in front of a human being on purpose. Where a normal keyboard has one shift key, the space-cadet has seven modifiers — Control, Meta, Super, Hyper (the famous "bucky bits"), plus Shift, Top, and a Front key labeled "Greek." They sit in stacked rows so your two hands can press several at once like a chord on a piano. Combine the modifiers and the shifts and a single fifty-key board produces up to *four thousand* distinct inputs.

Why would anyone want that? Because on a space-cadet keyboard, any command — no matter how complicated — is one gesture. You do not press Ctrl-K, Ctrl-X, Ctrl-C. You press *one* thing: that exact combination, memorized, done. The keyboard's whole philosophy is that the more keys you learn, the faster you go, and that a trained Lisp hacker would happily memorize thousands of single-character commands if it meant never lifting their hands or paging through a menu again. The Greeks are on the keys so you can type real mathematical notation — γ and Γ and the up-arrow — before Unicode existed. There was even a Macro key and four roman-numeral keys (I, II, III, IV) for picking from a four-item menu in one press.

The judgment was contested. Some users thought four thousand inputs was excess — a cockpit where you need a flight manual to type a letter. Others found it intoxicating: the whole system was just a single motion away. That attitude is exactly what shaped [Emacs](../exhibits/canon-cat/), which inherited the "M-" prefix from the space-cadet's Meta key and, decades later, ran on keyboards that turned every modifier into a separate idea.

Nobody needs four thousand keys. But the space-cadet is the museum's purest statement of a belief that computing has never quite let go of: that the user is not a beginner, that training is not a bug, and that the fastest interface in the world is the one you have already memorized. The rest of the [text-entry family](../exhibits/velotype/) is about shrinking the gap between thought and key. The space-cadet is the one that says the gap was never the problem — the *effort of reaching for the command* was.
