Every pointing device in my museum sits on a surface — until one doesn't. The
[Gyration GyroPoint](../exhibits/gyration-gyropoint/) was a mouse with the desk
struck out from under it. You didn't slide it. You waved it. And the reason it
worked is one of the most elegant tricks in the whole collection.

A gyroscope resists being turned. That's why a bicycle stays upright and why a
spinning top feels alive. The GyroPoint put a gyroscope inside a handheld unit:
a brushless motor spins a flywheel whose axle is held vertical by gravity, no
matter how you tilt the housing around it. Then it asks a quiet question — how
far did you tilt me? — and answers with light. Two pairs of gimbals carry
optical patterns; LEDs shine through them; quad photodiodes read how the
patterns shift as the housing rotates. Wrist up, cursor up. Wrist right, cursor
right. Not how far you moved, but how far you turned.

That inversion is the whole exhibit. A normal mouse is a position transducer:
two inches of plastic across a desk, two inches of arrow across a screen. The
GyroPoint is an orientation transducer: it doesn't care where you are, only
which way you're rotated. There's no desk, no pad, no receiver array, no
line-of-sight, no range limit. The patent, filed in March 1990 by Tom Quinn of
Gyration, spends real time explaining why this beats the ultrasonic systems of
the era — the ones that triangulate a wand against a receiver frame bolted to
the monitor. Ultrasonic tracking asks *where* your hand is. The GyroPoint asks
*which way it's turned*. Both answer "you gestured," but only one of them
carries the answer in its own pocket.

The company's founding product shipped in 1991 as a wireless RF pointer for
professional presenters. Gyration had been founded in June 1989 in Saratoga,
California, and its VP of R&D was a young engineer named Steve Hotelling — the
same Steve Hotelling who later gave Apple the multitouch vocabulary that made
the iPhone feel like a physical object. The line from a gyroscope you wave to a
screen you pinch is not a straight one, but it runs through the same obsession:
the device should feel your intent, not watch your position.

And yes — fifteen years later, in 2006, Nintendo put a version of this idea in
millions of living rooms. The Wii Remote's accelerometers sensed tilt and
motion; you swung it like a tennis racket and a tennis racket swung on screen.
The GyroPoint was inertial too, but it sensed rotation, not acceleration, using
a spinning wheel instead of a chip. The Wii Remote is the popular child of the
idea; the GyroPoint is the mechanical parent, still holding its flywheel up and
waiting for someone to wave.

I love this machine because it is the mouse paradigm seen from the inside out.
A mouse assumes you have a surface. The GyroPoint assumes you have a wrist — and
a hand attached to it, and the willingness to gesture like you mean it. The
whole apparatus of pointing, the most ordinary computer act of the last fifty
years, reduced to a spinning wheel that knows which way is down, and you.
