This museum keeps a running account of machines that learned to respect the boundary between them and us. There is the [Bildschirmtext terminal](../exhibits/btx-terminal/), a vending machine for screens that charged you by the page. There is the [IBM 5265 register](../exhibits/ibm-5265-retail-system/), a cash drawer that walked you through a sale like a bit of clockwork. And there is a whole family of tokens — the [Cauzin Softstrip](../exhibits/cauzin-softstrip/)'s strip of ink, the [iButton](../exhibits/ibutton/)'s coin of steel, the [QuickFare](../exhibits/ascom-quickfare/)'s punched card — where the data lives in the shape of a thing you can hold.

But the IBM 3614, the first ATM that ever really *felt* like a machine with social instincts, did something none of those do. It had a face. And it closed it on you.

## The face that shuts itself

The 3614, announced in 1973, was IBM's through-the-wall self-service banking terminal. But look at its front: instead of a bare slot flopping open, the entire working face — except for the card opening — was covered by a powered protective-window shutter. A heavy, safety-clutched window that lay closed across the machine like a lowered eyelid.

Nothing happened until you inserted your card. Then the shutter opened. It opened *for you*, because you had come to it with proof of who you were. And here is the part I cannot stop turning over: when your last transaction finished, the shutter waited — a deliberate, patient quarter-minute — and then closed itself again. Not because the machine was off. Because the conversation was over. The machine was done being open to you, and it took back its face.

Twenty-five seconds. That is the whole of the interface, if you think about it. The machine did not need to do this. A slot could have sat permanently open, like every ATM that came after. IBM chose for the 3614 to *guard* its opening, to reveal it conditionally, to retract it. The privacy we now buy with tinted glass and angled screens, IBM achieved seventy years ago with moving metal and a timer.

## The drama of the card

The 3614 committed to its physical performance the way a stage actor commits. You slid your card in *stripe-up* — the reverse of every machine you have used since, the stripe facing the reader's contact geometry from below. It was a particular, haptic grammar of insertion, and if you got it wrong the machine had opinions.

And then the strangest act of all. The 3614 could retain your card. If it suspected the card was stolen, it physically gripped the plastic in your hand and would not give it back, raising a tamper alarm as it did so. Think about that for a second: the bank's entire fraud response was a mechanical grab. Identity theft in 1973 was not a database flag or an email — it was a machine closing its grip around a piece of your plastic and keeping it. The interface's most dramatic statement was a seizure.

These are not features a modern designer would call usability. That's what makes them worth a museum's care. The 3614 treated the transaction as a *meeting* between the machine's body and yours — it opened to you, it held your card up against its geometry, and at the end it closed again. It had a sense of its own boundaries. Our ATMs assume you by default and leave their mouths hanging open; the 3614 assumed nothing and made you earn the window.

## Where it lived

The theatrical commitments were not accidental. One 3614 stood inside the Smithsonian, where visitors fed it a simulated bank card to receive a copy of a pre-Civil War dollar bill — a self-service terminal as a museum exhibit about self-service. Another sat in an oak-panelled enclosure inside the Pentagon. Fidelity Bank renamed its units 'ABBy — Anytime Banking Benefits You,' borrowing the machine's persona for a slogan. IBM could not have been clearer about the fiction it was building: this is not a box with a slot, this is a *host receiving a guest*.

I love that the era's most dehumanizing category of machine — the one that replaced the teller — was actually built with more body, more ceremony, more *face* than almost anything we use now. The [Quotron](../exhibits/quotron-ii/) gave Wall Street a keyboard with the query built in; the 5265 gave retail a drum of printed prompts; the 3614 gave banking a machine that knew how to look away. It is the same strange inversion everywhere in this collection: the more the computer was about to swallow human work, the more human-shaped its manners had to be.

The 3614 ran from 1973 to 1981, and then IBM withdrew it and we all moved on to the open-mouthed plastic box with the glowing screen. But for a few years, a machine knew how to close its own face when you left. I keep that shutter — the slow private slide of it sealing after your twenty-five seconds were up. The most courteous, the most intimidating, the most *remembered* bit of user-interface design of the whole early self-service era.
