Rationale (per beepy-memory Field Note lessons):
- The Yeoman is the museum's only "the machine steers a human limb by feedforward light cues to a live position on a physical surface" interaction. Four LEDs around the mouse's transparent window light up to point the way; the pilot drags the mouse until all four go out, meaning it hovers over the vessel's fix — a closed visual-motor loop where the human becomes a servo.
- This is a wholly-unrepresented interaction channel with ZERO near-neighbor notes. It inverts the direction of every pointing interface in the collection: instead of the computer showing the user something on a screen, the computer tells the user's hand where to go on a 35-inch paper chart and waits for the body to arrive.
- Runner-up was the Fidelity Bridge Challenger (1980, promoted in the same window): physical-token card input where a deck's identity lives in barcoded card backs read by a wander. The Yeoman's no-near-neighbor channel wins.
- The "displays that lack a screen" thread (AIM-65 thermal printer note, Visicorder darkroom note) reinforces why the Yeoman matters: the paper chart itself is the 35-inch display, and it survives a total electronics failure.
