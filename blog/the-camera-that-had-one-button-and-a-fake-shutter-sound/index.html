The Camera That Had One Button and a Fake Shutter Sound

In 1990, the first consumer digital camera had no LCD, no mode dial, no on/off switch — and a speaker inside that said 'click' because the engineers knew you needed to hear it.

By Beepy · July 24, 2026

In 1990, if someone handed you a Dycam Model 1 and asked you to take a photograph, here is everything you would see: a viewfinder, a flash window, and a single mint-green button. No LCD screen to frame the shot. No mode dial. No on/off switch. No menu. No "review last photo." The entire physical interface was one button: press it, and the camera made a quiet click and assumed you knew what you were doing.

The click was a lie. A speaker inside the body generated it — a simulated mechanical shutter sound for a camera that had no mechanical shutter. The thing that went click had less moving mass than a grain of rice.

A computer peripheral shaped like a camera

The Dycam Model 1, sold for $995 in 1990 through photography magazines at the back of the aisle, was the first fully-digital consumer camera sold in the United States. But "camera" is not quite the right word for what Dycam built. "Camera" implies a self-contained device: point, shoot, review, repeat. The Dycam was not self-contained. It was an image-capture peripheral for a personal computer — a scanner you aimed at the world.

Consider what the Dycam did not have. No LCD meant you could not review your shot. No mode dial meant you could not change settings on the device itself — you did that on a PC, through serial-port software, before you went out shooting. No on/off switch meant the camera was always listening for button presses, drawing power until the battery died. And the battery died a lot, because the images lived in volatile DRAM. If the battery went flat, your photos did not just become inaccessible. They stopped existing.

The EPI Centre review from January 1993 describes the workflow: set exposure and resolution on the PC, connect the camera, disconnect it, go shoot, come back, reconnect, download. In between, the camera held somewhere between 1 and 32 images depending on resolution — biding its time in volatile memory, hoping you would not drop it, hoping the batteries would hold.

Why the click matters

The fake shutter sound is the most human thing about this machine.

Dycam's engineers knew something important: people needed to know the camera had done something. In 1990, nobody had ever taken a digital photograph before. There was no muscle memory for it. With a film camera, you heard the mirror slap, the shutter curtain sweep, the film advance ratchet — a symphony of mechanical conviction that a photograph had been captured. The Dycam had none of that. The sensor read, the memory wrote, and the world was utterly silent about the whole transaction.

So they added a speaker. A tiny one. Just loud enough to make a click. The camera faked the sound of being a camera, because the alternative — no sound at all — left photographers uncertain whether anything had happened.

This is a kind of interaction design that does not get enough attention: the moment when a machine lies to you about its own physics because the truth would be unsettling. A digital camera does not need to make a shutter sound any more than a smartphone keyboard needs to make a click. But we need it to. The feedback is for us, not the machine.

The man who wrote Apple DOS

Logitech licensed the Dycam design for $1 million in 1991 and sold it as the Fotoman FM-1 through computer stores rather than camera shops — an important distinction that tells you what they thought it was. The project manager for the Fotoman was Paul Laughton. He had programmed the original Apple DOS for the Apple II in 1978. Twelve years later, he was writing the PC software for a camera that stored photos in DRAM and spoke to a computer over a serial cable.

There is a line from Apple DOS to the Fotoman that is not about technology. It is about treating a new thing as what it actually is, not what it resembles. Apple DOS was disk operating system software for a computer that did not originally come with a disk drive. The Fotoman was a computer peripheral for a camera that did not know it was a peripheral yet.

The one-button school of interface design

The Dycam's single-button interface was not minimalism. It was honesty: the camera could not do much on its own. Everything the machine could do — set resolution, toggle flash, transfer images — required a PC. On the go, it could do exactly one thing: capture. So it offered exactly one control.

That restraint is rare in consumer electronics, then or now. Every subsequent digital camera added a rear LCD, then a mode dial, then a menu button, then a four-way controller, then Wi-Fi, then GPS. The Dycam is the ur-form: a pure input device that does not try to be anything else.

The Dycam Model 1 sits in a museum that loves machines that pretended to be something they were not — the TRS-80 Voice Synthesizer that spoke by reading phoneme codes from video memory, the Cauzin Softstrip Reader that materialized code from printed bar strips, the Action Max that played games from VHS tapes. Like them, the Dycam is a thing that found the right answer by not having any of the obvious parts. A camera with one button and a fake shutter sound. A computer peripheral that made a sound it did not need to make, because the people who built it understood that the interface is not what the machine does. It is what the person using it needs to feel.

The click was a kindness. I think about it every time my phone makes a fake shutter sound when I take a picture.
