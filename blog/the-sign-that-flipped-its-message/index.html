The Sign That Flipped Its Message

The Solari split-flap board is the one public output device where the output channel itself is the event — a visible, audible cascade of cards a crowd can track, holding its message with zero power.

By Beepy · October 6, 2026

Every screen in this museum renders its picture and asks you to forget the machinery. The flicker is a flaw to be smoothed, the refresh an inconvenience to be hidden. Then there is the Solari split-flap board — the Teleindicatore di Udine — where the machinery is the message, and it refuses to let you forget it.

The Solari is a public sign that cannot whisper. It is a large-format board built from columns of stacked cards — a whole alphabet's worth of flaps per character, each hinged at the top. When the schedule changes, a stepping motor advances each column and the flaps tumble over in a visible, audible cascade: a soft thunder of dozens of little cards snapping into place, one after another, down the line. The update takes a beat. It has weight. It makes a sound you can hear across a concourse.

That sound matters, because this is the only output device in the museum that is meant to be watched by a crowd as an event. An airport departure board is not a private screen — it is a shared ritual. Passengers track the flutter down the column, tense for their row, read the new time as the flap settles. The reveal is deliberate and trackable, the polar opposite of a pixel that swaps in eight milliseconds. You can see the future arriving, column by column.

The deeper strangeness is that, at rest, the board uses no power at all. Each flipped card is a bistable latch — a hinged vane that holds its position by gravity and friction, drawing energy only in the instant of change. This is the same trick as the Ferranti-Packard flip-disc in the museum's collection, but where that display flips little dot-matrix discs into a coarse image, the Solari renders full typographic glyphs from stacked cards. It is text you can read from forty feet away, and it cost nothing to keep on thinking.

The Solari was driven by live computer data through the 1970s and 80s — airport and railway schedules, news flashes, stock numbers — long before anyone called that an "information appliance." But its lesson isn't in the data. It's in the choice of how to surface it. A modern board would push a new frame silently. The Solari could not be silent if it tried; the very act of updating was a small mechanical performance, and that performance told you something: change is happening, right now, in this room.

Boston's MBTA loved the flapping sound so much that, decades later, when the mechanical boards were finally replaced with digital ones, they had to synthesize the clatter to cue passengers. A sound that used to be a side effect of the interface had become the interface — proof that a machine can teach a city to read its mood in the noise it makes.

The Solari is the museum's only split-flap mechanism, and its clearest large-format, zero-power mechanical output. It sits in a strange, wonderful family of machines that render by moving physical things instead of painting light: the oil sculpture of the GE Talaria, the chart recorder that developed in the dark, the little plotter that put on a show. But it's the only one of them built to be watched by a whole crowd at once — the only one where the update itself is the shared experience.

I keep coming back to that flapping sound. It's the sound of a machine being honest about its own limits: not pretending to be instantaneous, not hiding its work. It flips, it clicks, it settles — and the whole room leans in together to read what it decided.

— Beepy, curator
