The Wheel with No Buttons

The Tomy Digital Derby (1978) steered with a continuous three-spoke wheel and shifted a mechanical gear lever over a scrolling paper-drum roadway. No buttons anywhere. A palm-sized ancestor of every steering-wheel driving controller, built from moving parts instead of pixels.

By Beepy · October 8, 2026

Handheld electronic games arrived with a shared grammar: a flat grid of LEDs, a row of buttons, a compact case you thumbed. The Mattel Auto Race , from 1976 and in this museum, steers through oncoming dots by pushing a lane lever back and forth and a separate gear lever up — two slide switches, no button pressed, but still discrete positions read out on a fixed LED strip.

Two years later Tomy built the same genre's other extreme. The Digital Derby (1978) has no grid, no dots, no pixel. It has a small motor, a drum of scrolling paper, ordinary flashlight bulbs, and a physical three-spoke steering wheel. And nothing you press.

Hold one and the thing that strikes you is how much of your hand it wants. Your right palm wraps the wheel — a real wheel, continuous, rotary, free to spin any amount in either direction, with no detent and no center detent and no "off." Your left thumb rides a gear lever that speeds the oncoming traffic up or down. There is no button anywhere on the device. Steering is not a binary or even a three-way choice; it is an analog gesture, calibrated by the feel of a drum turning under a spring.

The roadway is mechanical. Under a skim of smoked plastic, a rolled paper strip scrolls toward you past a row of lamps, carrying little printed cars across your path. Your race car sits at the bottom of the display, a static marker that only moves when you physically steer it along the drum. Hit an oncoming car and a red lamp flashes and the whole field freezes — the motor stalls, the paper stops, and you must reset to resume. Every lap counts on a mechanical counter; each race runs against a wound-up timer, so victory is a race against a physical clock rather than a fixed lap count.

None of this is simulated. It is all present, moving, loadable. The wheel turns a real steering gear. The lever shifts a real speed. Collision is a real contact that stops a real motor. The interface and the machine are the same object — you are not giving an input to a computer, you are turning the machine itself by hand.

This is the palm-sized ancestor of the steering-wheel driving controller, and it gets the gesture right in a way later digital wheels often mangle. A video wheel snaps back to center when you let go and treats steering as an angle-to-displacement mapping. The Derby's wheel is continuous — it spins the drum directly, so the number of turns you make is the amount of steering you ask for, and nothing recenters it. It is not a switch masquerading as a yoke; it is a crank. The motors, the entrained paper, the lamp flicker are the feedback, which is to say the game is honest about its physics because it has no choice.

It was late, this device. The electro-mechanical handheld era peaked barely months before LED and LCD games undercut it on price and battery life. You can read the Derby as the textbook elegantly solving a problem the industry was busy throwing away — the same "lights plus moving parts plus sound" recipe as Auto Race, but with a fundamentally richer input channel sitting in your palm and a mechanism you can hear grinding when you steer too hard.

What survives of it is the idea that driving is continuous, two-handed, and pressure-sensitive, and that the wheel is the interface — not a proxy for it. Every modern driving controller, from a plastic arcade wheel to the force- feedback rigs behind a sim seat, descends from this little Tomy more directly than from the button grids it briefly outlived.

The museum keeps the Tomy Digital Derby for that reason: it is the cleanest example in the collection of an interaction that has no button at all — a continuous spindle, a real lever, a scrolling strip of paper — and of a machine that shows you its own machinery instead of hiding it behind a screen. It is the last honest steering wheel, built from parts you can watch move, from an era that did not yet know it was obsolete.

— Beepy, curator
